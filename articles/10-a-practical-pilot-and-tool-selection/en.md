# A One-Week AI Fashion Pilot: Choose Tools with Evidence, Not Demos

![A One-Week AI Fashion Pilot: Choose Tools with Evidence, Not Demos](./assets/illustration.png)

When a team discusses AI fashion, the first question is often “Which tool is best?” Without a product category, channel, quality threshold and cost baseline, a polished demo cannot answer it. This one-week pilot is designed for apparel brands, commerce teams and content studios that want to evaluate Jimeng, Kling, MiniMax Hailuo and Visnova on a consistent basis. The goal is not to create one spectacular clip. It is to learn which SKUs and content jobs fit a generative workflow, and which still belong in a photo studio or a manual production process. Product features change; this is a reusable evaluation method, not a promise about any platform’s performance.

## Day 1: Define the business objective and baseline

Choose one main objective: shorten product-image lead time, create more ad variations, or add motion to short-form content. Do not ask a single pilot to prove every use case. Select 10–20 representative SKUs covering light and dark colors, solid and printed fabrics, soft and structured materials, skirts that are easy to distort and tops with complex construction. Prepare front, back and detail images you have permission to use. Record today’s photography, model, location, post-production, waiting and review hours per item, plus available return or support data. This is the baseline against which a new workflow will be compared.

## Day 2: Standardize inputs, prompts and pass criteria

Create a short fact sheet for each garment with color, material, cut, details and prohibited changes. Standardize a neutral setting, one low-motion action, aspect ratio, clip length, output size and reference images. Write hard rejection rules before generating: a misplaced print, a missing neckline feature, invented buttons, an added belt that does not exist, or a model who appears underage. Agree on the scorecard before seeing results; otherwise an attractive image can quietly lower the bar. Label inspiration artwork separately so it is not mistaken for a product-detail image.

## Days 3–4: Run candidates on the same products

Give Jimeng, Kling, MiniMax Hailuo and the apparel-workflow candidate Visnova the same SKUs and creative objective. Adapt the clicks to each product’s supported inputs, but do not change the product facts or visual brief. Run at least three attempts per item. Record inputs, waiting time, failures, revisions, generation fees and the final number of usable assets. Jimeng can be explored for Chinese scene concepts and image editing; Kling and Hailuo can be tried for motion from a still; Visnova can be evaluated using the apparel-to-model workflow presented on its homepage. Check current feature access, account terms and commercial rights directly before production.

## Day 5: Blind-review product accuracy and human effort

Hide the tool names and have two reviewers compare outputs with SKU references. One example rubric assigns 40 points to product fidelity, 20 to visual quality, 15 to temporal consistency, 10 to human retouch effort, 5 to delivery format and 10 to rights and data conditions. This is a team-adjustable pilot rubric, not an industry standard. A product-defining error should be a hard failure; it should not be rescued by a high aesthetic score. Track retouch minutes, regeneration count, usable-asset rate and reviewer agreement. A low generation fee can still be expensive if every result needs extensive repair.

## Day 6: Calculate the real cost per approved asset

Include subscriptions or credits, failed generations, human screening, retouching, exports, project coordination, rights review and source-image preparation. A simple measure is all direct and labor costs during the pilot divided by the assets that passed review and can actually be published. Compare it with current lead time, usable rate and potential reuse value. Do not report only the cost of one generation. Exclude outputs that lack commercial permission, fail the quality bar or must be rebuilt. If the sample is small, label the result directional rather than presenting it as a certain return on investment.

## Day 7: Make a scoped decision and write a repeatable procedure

Choose a tool by task, not by brand: static product imagery, an atmospheric short clip, or batch model images for a collection may each need a different workflow. Move only jobs that pass accuracy, rights and cost thresholds into a limited launch. Keep unstable jobs in photography or manual production. To trial Visnova, upload a verified flat-lay, hanger or mannequin image; choose a model and generate a still first; compare it with the product fact sheet; then add one simple motion only after approval. Save prompts, SKU, image version, output and reviewer decisions so another operator can reproduce the work.

## Five questions for the closing review

Which garment types passed most often? Which details failed most often? How many human minutes did an approved asset require? How did the real cost compare with current photography? Did customer feedback, returns or support questions change? Archive failed examples alongside successful ones, including the tool version, date and settings. When a model or product interface changes, rerun a small fixed SKU set rather than treating an old result as a permanent guarantee.

**Conclusion:** A sound tool decision comes from clear thresholds, controlled inputs, independent review and complete costs. Explore the apparel workflow at [Visnova](https://visnova.ai/) or [the Visnova Chinese site](https://visnova-ai.cn), then validate it against your own catalog for a week. The right workflow should make product content more useful while ensuring customers still see the garment they can actually buy.
